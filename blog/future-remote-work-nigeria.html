Data Privacy for Students and Young Creators

By Digital Nasarawa Innovation Hub · Privacy & Safety

Young people increasingly live their lives online. They are creating profiles, posting content, joining communities, making purchases, university applications, and building digital identities before they fully understand the consequences of sharing personal data. The challenge is not that digital participation is bad; it is that many people do not understand how much personal information can be exposed through everyday online activity.

Data privacy is the practice of controlling what information is shared, who can see it, and how it is used. For students and creators, this includes names, contact details, locations, school identities, voice recordings, photos, personal routines, and even behavioral patterns. These details can be collected by platforms, advertisers, apps, or bad actors. If privacy is not handled carefully, it can lead to fraud, stalking, impersonation, loss of trust, or embarrassing online exposure.

One reason this topic is so important is that many young people do not believe personal data can be harmful until something clearly goes wrong. This is not a safe way to approach digital life. Most online risks are built from small, repeated patterns: oversharing, weak passwords, accepting unknown app permissions, or using public social media accounts without filtering what is visible. When these habits accumulate, the risks increase.

Creators are especially vulnerable

Young creators often need to share content to grow an audience, build a portfolio, or attract clients. That is positive and necessary. But content creation often involves public visibility, which can expose personal details unintentionally. A creator may share a school name, family details, phone number, event location, or a routine schedule that an outsider can use to infer more than intended. The goal is not to eliminate online visibility, but to manage it deliberately.

Creators should learn to separate public content from personal information. A public profile may need to show the work, but it does not need to display home addresses, sensitive family information, or consistent location patterns. Content calendars, brand identity, and privacy settings can support safe growth without sacrificing authentic expression. Digital creativity does not require recklessness.

Apps and platform permissions matter

Many apps request access to contacts, microphones, location, camera roll, or notifications. Students often grant permissions without reading what is requested. This may be useful for some apps, but it creates unnecessary data exposure in others. It is wise to review permissions regularly and remove apps that ask for more access than needed. If an app is not essential, it should not be treated as harmless because it is small or popular.

Privacy also depends on account security. Students should use strong, unique passwords and enable two-factor authentication. They should avoid logging into apps or websites from unknown or compromised devices. These simple protections prevent a lot of digital damage and reduce the risk of account takeover, identity misuse, and data theft.

Data privacy is a digital citizenship skill

Students should be taught that privacy is not secrecy; it is control. It is about making informed choices about what is shared and with whom. This is a core part of digital citizenship. People who understand privacy are better equipped to protect themselves, handle information responsibly, and resist manipulation. They also become more aware of how institutions, apps, and marketers use their data.

Schools and families can teach this through regular practice. Students can learn to adjust privacy settings, review contact lists, avoid public post patterns, and think carefully before sharing information. These are habits that shape how digital identities evolve. A young person who starts building privacy awareness early is more likely to carry it into adulthood, when the consequences of digital exposure can become much bigger.

Data privacy is not a technical burden; it is a life skill. It allows students and creators to express themselves online without giving away too much. It supports safer communities and better trust. In a digital ecosystem where information moves quickly, control matters. Digital confidence should not come at the cost of digital vulnerability. Privacy is one of the foundations that makes healthy online growth possible.
